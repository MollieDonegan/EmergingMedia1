Week 4

My art practice centres on class and public space, so I found Claire Bishop's Artificial Hells particularly useful. Working with socially engaged and participatory methods has made me interested in how these practices have developed and what we can learn from earlier examples of people using art and performance to come together. I was especially interested in Bishop's discussion of Proletkult theatre in post-revolutionary Russia and the idea of working-class people creating their own culture, rather than having it produced or defined by others.

From first reading Marx and Engels' The Communist Manifesto I began to understand how class shapes our everyday lives, including who has access to resources, who gets heard and who gets to make culture. Access is something I keep coming back to in my own work. Who gets to use public space? Whose experiences are represented? And who gets the opportunity to make art, rather than just experience it? I also find myself wondering how we can create something different when so much of what we understand about art and culture has been shaped by the systems we are trying to challenge. Even when we work collectively, we bring our existing ideas about power, authority and whose opinions matter with us.

What interested me about Proletkult was its attempt to create a culture by and for the working class. Alexander Bogdanov, one of the movement's key figures, believed that collective creative work could offer an alternative to bourgeois individualism. I was drawn to the idea of people producing their own culture and expressing their experiences in their own terms, rather than having someone else speak for them. This feels relevant to my interest in public space and how it might become a place where people can share their experiences and challenge the ways they are represented.

However, Bishop also explores some of the problems with this idea. Bogdanov's vision of a collective culture could become quite restrictive, with individual experiences and differences pushed aside in favour of a shared political vision. Leon Trotsky questioned aspects of this approach and placed greater importance on individual experience. I found this tension interesting because it made me question whether bringing people together is enough to create change. If people are invited to participate but are expected to think or act in a particular way, how much freedom do they really have? And who gets to decide what the collective represents?

These are questions I think about in my own practice. Just because people come together to make something does not mean that power is shared equally. The artist might still decide how a project works, what people can contribute and which ideas are ultimately shown or heard. I am interested in how a project can develop through the people involved, rather than everything being decided in advance. I don't think collaboration automatically gets rid of existing hierarchies, but I want to understand how these might be questioned through the process of making work together.

In Martha Rosler's interview with PIN-UP, Rosler discusses the housing crisis and the displacement taking place in the city. She also raises an interesting contradiction: artists can be affected by gentrification while also contributing to it. This made me think about the position of art and artists within the very systems they might be trying to challenge, and how cultural spaces can sometimes contribute to the inequalities they claim to address.

What I find interesting about If You Lived Here is that Rosler did not simply make an exhibition about homelessness. She brought together artists, homeless people, activists, architects and community groups through exhibitions and public discussions, creating a space for people to share their experiences and think about alternative ways of organising housing and the city. The project took place at Dia Art Foundation, an established art institution in a neighbourhood experiencing gentrification. I find this particularly interesting because Rosler used an existing art space to question the social and economic structures surrounding it.

Both of the reading examples this week have made me think more critically about participation. I don't want to assume that bringing people together or giving them a platform is enough in itself. I'm interested in what happens when people can influence a project, shape the conversations around it and decide what they want to come out of it. For me, the question is not just how to make culture more accessible, but how we might change the way it is produced and who gets to shape it. I am still trying to understand how I can work collectively without reproducing the same hierarchies we are trying to move away from, and how public space might offer a place to begin exploring this.
