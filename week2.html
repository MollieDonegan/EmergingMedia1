Week 2

At the moment I am reading Susan Sontag and doing this week's reading for class made me think about how her ideas relate to Walter Benjamin's writing on mechanical reproduction. Both are thinking about what happens when images can be reproduced and circulated beyond the original thing or event, but they approach it in different ways. It made me think about how the reproduction and circulation of images changes the way we experience art and the world around us. Benjamin's argument that mechanical reproduction changes the function of art feels particularly relevant to photography because an image can be separated from the original object, place, or event and circulated far beyond its original context. He writes that "photography freed the hand of the most important artistic functions which henceforth devolved only upon the eye looking into a lens." Photography makes it possible for images to be produced and shared at a scale that was not previously possible, changing who can access images and how they are experienced.

Sontag's idea that "To collect photographs is to collect the world" made me think about what happens when the world becomes something that can constantly be photographed, stored and consumed through images. Photography can give us access to people, places and events that we may never physically encounter, but this access is complicated. An image can make something feel available to us while removing it from its original context. This seems particularly important when thinking about photographs of violence or suffering. We can encounter an image of an event repeatedly through newspapers, social media or archives, but the ability to see something does not necessarily mean that we understand it or have any relationship to the people involved.

This connects strongly to Benjamin's discussion of mass movements, particularly his argument that "Mass reproduction is aided especially by the reproduction of masses." He describes how photography and film can capture parades, rallies, sporting events and war in ways that the individual eye cannot. He writes that "Mass movements are usually discerned more clearly by a camera than by the naked eye." The camera therefore does not simply record an event. It changes what can be seen and how that event can be understood by people who were not physically there.

I think this raises questions about access and ethics that are important to the way I think about art. Reproduction can make art, information and political events more accessible, allowing people to encounter things that would otherwise be restricted by geography, institutions or physical access. At the same time, making something accessible through an image does not make the process neutral. Someone is still deciding what is photographed, how it is framed, where it is circulated and how people are asked to look at it. This is especially complicated when the subject is another person's experience or suffering. This makes me think about art as something that can exist outside of a gallery or a single physical object. Reproduction can create new forms of access and allow images to move between different communities and spaces. However, I think the ethics of that circulation are just as important as the access it creates. Benjamin helps explain how technologies of reproduction change the scale and function of images, while Sontag makes me think more about what it means to consume those images and what can be lost when an experience becomes something we can simply look at and move on from.
